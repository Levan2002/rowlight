How to increase or decrease evenly across a row

"Increase 6 stitches evenly across the row" is one of the most annoying lines in a pattern. It never tells you where. The good news is that the maths is simple once you know the trick, and you can do it with a pencil in about a minute. Here's the method, two worked examples, and a few things that trip people up.

The idea in one sentence

Divide the stitches that aren't changing into as many equal groups as you have increases or decreases, then work one increase or decrease after each group.

Increasing evenly: worked example

Say you have 48 stitches and need to increase 6 evenly. Each increase is a new stitch that sits between the existing ones, so you're spacing 6 increases through 48 stitches.

  1. Divide the stitches you have by the number of increases: 48 ÷ 6 = 8.
  2. Work 8 stitches, then increase. Repeat that 6 times. That uses 6 × 8 = 48 stitches and adds 6, for a total of 54.

Sometimes the number doesn't divide neatly. With 50 stitches and 6 increases, 50 ÷ 6 is 8 with 2 left over. Make most groups 8 and a couple of them 9, spread them out rather than putting them all at one end. A common version is "(k8, M1) 4 times, (k9, M1) 2 times".

Working flat (back and forth), it's usual to keep the first and last stitches plain, so that the changes don't land on the selvedge. Many people therefore start with half a group, space the changes evenly, and finish with the other half. Working in the round there are no edges, so you simply repeat the groups all the way round.

Decreasing evenly

Decreases use up two stitches each (for example, knit two together), so the groups are a little smaller. To decrease 6 stitches across 48:

  1. Each decrease uses 2 stitches, so 6 decreases use 12 of the 48. That leaves 36 plain stitches.
  2. Divide the plain stitches by the number of decreases: 36 ÷ 6 = 6.
  3. Work 6 plain stitches, then decrease. Repeat 6 times. That's 6 × (6 + 2) = 48 stitches used and 42 on the needle.

The same logic works in crochet. An increase is usually two stitches in one stitch; a decrease is a "2tog" style stitch, such as sc2tog, which joins two stitches into one. In crochet an increase uses up one stitch from the row below, a decrease uses up two.

Which increase should you use?

The method tells you where; the pattern should say which increase or decrease. If it names one (for example "kfb" or "M1L"), use it. If it doesn't, a make-one (M1) is one of the least visible in knitting, and two single crochets in the next stitch is the standard in crochet. Use the same stitch for every change so the fabric looks consistent.

Mistakes to watch for

  • Mixing up how many stitches each change uses. A make-one uses none of the stitches below, a knit-front-and-back or two single crochets in one stitch uses one, and a decrease uses two. The sums differ, so check your count after the row.
  • Putting the changes at the edge. Edge increases and decreases curl and are hard to seam. Keep a plain stitch or two at each end of a flat row.
  • Stacking them in a column. If you repeat the same spacing on every shaping row, the changes line up and form a visible seam. Offset the positions by a few stitches next time.
  • Forgetting to recount. After the row, count your stitches. If the total isn't what the pattern says, find out now, not 10 rows later.

Doing it with Rowlight

If you'd rather not do the arithmetic every time, Rowlight has an Increase or decrease evenly tool on the Tools tab. It handles knitting and crochet, flat and in the round, and writes the instructions out for you.

  1. Open Tools and tap Increase or decrease evenly (the screen is titled Increase Evenly).
  2. At the top, choose your craft (Knitting or Crochet), whether you're making an Increase or a Decrease, and whether you're working Flat (rows) or In the round.
  3. Type your current number of stitches in Stitches now, and how many you need to add or remove in Stitches to increase (or to decrease).
  4. Read the result under Instructions. For 48 stitches with 6 increases, knitting, it gives you a line you can follow directly, plus a note like "48 stitches become 54". A short legend explains the abbreviations (K = knit, M1 = make one, K2tog = knit two together).
  5. If you ask for more changes than the row can hold, the tool tells you the most that will fit.

The tool uses standard stitches (M1 and K2tog for knitting; sc, two sc in the next stitch and sc2tog for crochet). If your pattern names a different increase, use that one in the same positions. Rowlight remembers the last numbers you entered, so you can adjust them as you move through a pattern.
