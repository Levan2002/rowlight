How to keep track of rows in a PDF pattern

Most patterns are PDFs now, and most of us read them on a phone or tablet. That makes it easy to lose your place: the screen locks, you scroll a little, the dog needs out, and suddenly you can't tell if you're on row 14 or row 18. Here are the ways people keep their place in a PDF pattern, and what tends to go wrong with each.

Why it's harder than it looks

On paper you'd tick the row off with a pencil. A PDF has nothing to write on, so the information about where you are lives in your head, or in a separate counter that doesn't know anything about the page. When the two disagree (and after a phone call, they will), you spend ten minutes counting stitches backwards to work out which row you're on.

The fix is to keep your place on the pattern itself, not only as a number somewhere else. A number tells you "row 18"; a marker on the page shows you which line that is.

Six ways to keep your place

1. A separate row counter

A clicker ring or a counter app is simple and works for any pattern. Its weakness is that it only holds a number. If you set the work down for a week, you still have to find row 18 on the page, and you have to trust that the number is right.

2. Highlight the row in a PDF markup app

Most PDF apps let you highlight a line. It keeps your place on the page, but you have to move the highlight by hand every row, which is fiddly with yarn in one hand, and old highlights pile up and make the page hard to read.

3. Print it and tick it off

Paper is reliable, and a pencil tick is hard to beat. It costs ink and paper, and it's awkward for long patterns and large charts.

4. Turn it into a checklist

Copy the rows into a notes app and check them off. This works well for written patterns with clear "Row 5:" or "Rnd 3:" lines. It's slow to set up, and retyping risks typos that change a stitch count.

5. A sticky note or a pin in the work

Write the row you just finished on a sticky note at the top of the page, or pin a safety pin or removable marker into the fabric at the end of a known row. These are good back-ups when you put a project down for a while.

6. A counter that moves a marker for you

The most convenient option combines the first two: one tap adds a row to the counter and moves a highlight to the next line, so the page and the number can't drift apart. That's what Rowlight does, described below.

Habits that help, whichever method you use

  • Count a row when you finish it, not when you start it. If the counter says 18, rows 1 to 18 are done and you're working on 19. Pick one rule and stick to it.
  • Stop at the end of a row. Putting work down mid-row is how you end up unsure which side you're on. If you must stop, note the stitch you reached.
  • Fix the counter when you rip back. After undoing rows, take the counter back by the same number straight away, before you forget.
  • Use a second counter for repeats. If a stitch pattern repeats every 8 rows, a separate repeat counter ("row 3 of 8") is easier than counting up to 96.
  • Read charts the right way. Charts are normally read from the bottom row upwards. For flat knitting, right-side rows are usually read right to left and wrong-side rows left to right; the pattern's key will tell you.

Common mistakes

The most common is counting the foundation chain or the cast-on as a row; most patterns don't, so check how yours numbers rows. The next is forgetting to move the marker or counter after an interruption. If the pattern has stitch counts in brackets, such as "(24 sts)", use them to confirm your row before a small mistake grows.

Doing it with Rowlight

Rowlight is an iPhone app built for this one job. You import the PDF, put a highlighter bar on your row, and every tap on the + button adds a row to the counter and moves the bar to the next one. Here's how to set it up.

  1. Import the pattern. In Projects, tap + and choose PDF from Files. You can also choose Photos or Screenshots, or Scan Paper Pattern for a printed page. If the PDF is in Safari, Mail or a shop's download page, share it to Rowlight instead. Rowlight keeps its own copy; your original file is never changed.
  2. Place the bar on your current row. Tap the pattern where your row is and the highlighter bar jumps there. Drag its bottom edge until it covers exactly one row. You can also open More (the ellipsis) › Highlighter and use Thinner and Taller under Bar height.
  3. Tap + when you finish a row. The big + button (Next Row) at the bottom raises the row counter and moves the bar one bar-height along. The − button (Previous Row) goes back. Prefer it on the left? Turn on Left-handed counter in Settings.
  4. Choose the direction. Written patterns read from the top down, which is the default. For a chart read from the bottom up, open Highlighter and set Direction to Up (charts).
  5. Jump if you're out of sync. Tap the row number in the counter to open Go to Row and type the row you're really on. The bar stays where it is, so tap the pattern to move it too. If you lose the bar after scrolling, tap Find Bar (the target icon beside Highlighter) to bring it back into view.
  6. Dim everything else, if you like. In the Highlighter sheet, turn on Focus mode to dim the page except for the current row.
  7. Add a reminder. From More › Row Reminders, add something like "At row 24: start the decreases". When your counter reaches that row, Rowlight shows it and gives a small buzz.

Rowlight remembers your place and zoom in each project, so you can close the app and come back next week to the same row. One honest limit: the bar moves by its own height each tap, so it works best on patterns whose rows are evenly spaced, like charts and tidy typeset rows. For text patterns with lines of different lengths, use the Steps checklist (the checklist icon at the top) instead. Rowlight splits the pattern text into rows and rounds, keeping the designer's wording exactly as written.

Rowlight doesn't read chart symbols for you. It keeps your place and your progress on the chart as it is.
