US vs UK crochet terms: a stitch-by-stitch conversion chart

A US "double crochet" and a UK "double crochet" are two different stitches. If you've ever followed a pattern and ended up with something much tighter or looser than the picture, mixed-up terms are a likely reason. This guide shows the two systems side by side, how to tell which one your pattern uses, and how to convert a pattern without errors.

Why the same name means two stitches

Crochet in the UK and in the US grew up with different names for the basic stitches. For any given stitch, the UK name is one step up the ladder from the US name: the stitch US crocheters call a single crochet is a double crochet in UK terms, a US double crochet is a UK treble, and so on. Nothing is wrong with either system. The trouble is that patterns are sold worldwide, and many don't say which one they use.

Notice the pattern: from single crochet upwards, each US name matches the next name along in the UK list. Chain and slip stitch are the same in both (only the abbreviation of slip stitch differs).

How to tell which system a pattern uses

  • Check the notes at the start. Many designers write "US terms" or "UK terms" near the materials list, or include a stitch glossary.
  • Look for a stitch that only exists in one system. If a pattern uses a "single crochet", it's US terms. UK patterns don't use that name, and if they mention "half treble" (htr) or "miss", they're UK.
  • Look at the spelling and the vocabulary. "Tension", "colour" and "miss" point to a UK designer, though that isn't proof. "Gauge" and "skip" point to the US.
  • Look at the picture and the designer's site. The designer's blog or shop page often states which terms they use, and if in doubt, ask them.

How to convert a pattern safely

Converting by hand sounds simple, but there is a trap: if you change every "dc" to "tr" and then every "tr" to "dtr" as a second find-and-replace, you convert some stitches twice. The only safe method is to go through the pattern once, left to right, changing each stitch name a single time.

Practical tips:

  • Convert one row at a time and cross it off, rather than doing a find-and-replace on the whole text.
  • Don't forget the abbreviations that hide inside other words: "2 dc tog", "sc2tog", "FPdc" (front post) and "BPdc" (back post) all contain a stitch name.
  • Remember that "miss" in UK patterns is "skip" in US patterns, and "tension" is "gauge".
  • Swatch before you commit. If your swatch matches the pattern's stated gauge and looks like the photo, the terms are right.

A quick example

Take the US line "Rnd 2: ch 3, 2 dc in same st, sc in next st". In UK terms it reads "Rnd 2: ch 3, 2 tr in same st, dc in next st". The chain stays the same, the US double crochets become treble crochets, and the US single crochet becomes a double crochet. If you read a UK "dc" as a US double crochet, you'd work a taller stitch than the designer intended, and the piece would come out looser and bigger than the photo.

Doing it with Rowlight
